## Section 2: Target Ranges for Total and Free Testosterone in Women

**What is publicly documented:**

Misleh does not state specific numerical targets in any free public content reviewed. Specific targets, if taught, are locked behind the paid course. What can be attributed to him from public content:

- He argues standard commercial laboratory reference ranges for female testosterone are unreliable, built from a "population that includes symptomatic women" and therefore cannot differentiate normal from optimal values. This is a scientifically legitimate critique — it is acknowledged in the clinical literature.
- He emphasizes free testosterone over total testosterone as the clinically relevant fraction, with SHBG context required to interpret total T values.
- One publicly accessible HRT University article references the equilibrium dialysis (gold standard) free testosterone range for young women as approximately **3–6 ng/dL**, though this is citing assay methodology rather than setting a specific treatment target.
- One search engine summary of Episode 50 described Misleh claiming "new data suggests optimal is up to 30x higher than you think" relative to standard reference ranges. This framing is consistent with the functional medicine community's argument that current low reference ranges (based on contemporary populations, often 8–30 ng/dL for total T) should be compared against historical premenopausal norms from the 1970s–1980s literature (~70–90 ng/dL in young women), yielding a fold-difference argument. This is a contested interpretation; the academic literature does not endorse supraphysiologic targets for women.
- His stated dosing philosophy: **"the maximum effective dose, not the smallest one that moves a lab value"** — this is explicitly in tension with the consensus "minimum effective dose" principle.

**What cannot be verified from public content:**

- Specific numerical targets for total or free testosterone used clinically
- Whether his targets stay within or exceed the premenopausal physiologic reference range (15–70 ng/dL total T by LC-MS/MS, or 0.5–2.4 nmol/L)
- Whether he adjusts targets by menopausal status or age

## Section 3: Preferred Route and Specific Dosing

**Route — publicly documented:**

Misleh is explicitly and publicly **anti-pellet** for women. This position is stated in at least two formats (blog/podcast): pellets "can release sustained, sometimes high, testosterone levels that cannot be rapidly reversed if side effects emerge." He favors adjustability and reversibility.

His stated preferred routes:
1. **Compounded topical creams and gels** — "allow titration and are less invasive"
2. **Injections (IM and subcutaneous)** — "highly controllable and effective"; he discusses IM vs. subQ head-to-head data, 27-gauge half-inch needles for subQ, and site rotation

His position on compounded preparations is a direct divergence from the 2019 Global Consensus Statement, which specifically advises against compounded bioidentical testosterone due to insufficient efficacy and safety data.

**Specific dosing — publicly documented:**

None. No specific mg doses for women appear in any free public content. The paid course curriculum states it covers "dosing," but those specifics are not publicly available.

## Section 8: Evidence Quality for Each Point of Divergence

Evidence hierarchy applied: **RCT/meta-analysis > consensus guideline (evidence-synthesized) > prospective observational > retrospective observational > expert opinion/clinical experience**

---

### Divergence 1: Indications Beyond HSDD

**Consensus position (HSDD only):**
- Supported by: 2019 Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology systematic review and meta-analysis (Islam et al.) — 36 RCTs, 8,480 participants. Demonstrated significant benefit for sexual desire, arousal, orgasm, and frequency of satisfying sexual events. Found effects on "musculoskeletal and cognitive health warrant further investigation."
- Supported by: The 2019 Global Consensus Position Statement itself, endorsed by Endocrine Society, NAMS, ISSWSH, International Menopause Society, EMAS, ISSM.
- 2025 update: A 2025 review titled "Testosterone in women: beyond hypoactive sexual desire" concluded that while biological plausibility exists for muscle, bone, and cognitive benefits, RCT evidence is insufficient to support prescribing for these indications.
- A 2025 presentation at The Menopause Society Annual Meeting concluded: "there is no justification for treating postmenopausal women or premenopausal women with testosterone to prevent muscle loss, bone fracture, heart disease, depression, cognitive decline, or fatigue" based on current RCT data.
- **Evidence quality for consensus (HSDD-only) position: Level 1 (RCT meta-analysis + consensus guideline)**

**Misleh's expanded indications position:**
- Supported by: Biological plausibility — testosterone receptors exist in brain, muscle, and bone; observational studies show associations between testosterone levels and lean mass, BMD, and cognitive performance.
- A 2024 pilot study (Sisk et al., published 2025) found transdermal testosterone improved mood and cognitive symptoms; authors explicitly stated further RCTs are needed.
- Associational data from the Cardiovascular Health Study (Orwoll et al., JCEM 2011) showed higher free testosterone in older women associated with greater BMD and lean mass — cross-sectional, not RCT evidence.
- **Evidence quality for Misleh's expanded indications: Level 3–4 (observational + pilot study data + expert clinical experience). No RCTs confirm benefit for fatigue, mood, cognition, or musculoskeletal outcomes as primary endpoints at physiologic dosing in women.**

**Verdict on Divergence 1:** The consensus position is the higher-evidence position. Misleh's expanded indications are plausible physiologically and supported by emerging data, but not yet confirmed by adequately powered RCTs. Treating women for fatigue, muscle, bone, or cognitive indications based on testosterone is currently off-label beyond guideline and rests on expert opinion and observational data.

---

### Divergence 2: Premenopausal Women as Treatment Population

**Consensus position (postmenopausal only):**
- RCT data on testosterone in women was obtained almost exclusively from postmenopausal and surgically menopausal populations. The consensus did not extend recommendations to premenopausal women because the evidence base did not include them.
- **Evidence quality: Level 1 for postmenopausal population; Level 5 (absence of evidence) for premenopausal**

**Misleh's position (any symptomatic woman):**
- No RCT evidence in premenopausal women identified. Misleh's "universal female testosterone screening" and implied treatment of premenopausal women rests on physiologic reasoning and clinical experience only.
- **Evidence quality: Expert opinion / clinical experience. Level 5.**

**Verdict on Divergence 2:** Treating premenopausal women with testosterone for the indications Misleh lists is not supported by RCT evidence. It is not categorically unsafe at physiologic doses based on available data, but "no evidence of harm" at short follow-up is not the same as evidence of benefit.

---

### Divergence 3: Dosing Philosophy — Maximum Effective Dose vs. Minimum Effective Dose

**Consensus position (minimize dose):**
- The Lancet 2019 meta-analysis established safety and efficacy data at physiologic doses (approximating premenopausal normal range). Supraphysiologic dosing was not included in any RCT meeting inclusion criteria.
- Androgenic side effects (acne, hair growth, voice changes, clitoromegaly — some irreversible) occur at supraphysiologic concentrations.
- **Evidence quality: Level 1 for physiologic dosing safety; risk at supraphysiologic doses established by clinical data.**

**Misleh's position (maximize dose to symptom response):**
- His stated philosophy is to target the "maximum effective dose" rather than the minimal dose that moves a lab value. This is explicitly stated in public podcast content.
- At physiologic replacement doses, his approach may be consistent with consensus if his actual targets stay within premenopausal physiologic range; however, his critique of reference ranges and "30x higher than you think" framing raises concerns about whether targets remain physiologic.
- **Evidence quality: Expert clinical judgment. Level 5. Not supported by RCT data.**

**Verdict on Divergence 3:** At physiologic doses, the difference may be more rhetorical than clinical. If Misleh's "maximum effective dose" remains within the premenopausal physiologic range (~15–70 ng/dL total T), safety data support the approach. If it exceeds that range, no long-term RCT safety data exist. The 2019 Lancet meta-analysis explicitly noted that safety data beyond 24 months and at supraphysiologic levels are absent.

---

### Divergence 4: Compounded Preparations

**Consensus position (do not recommend compounded testosterone):**
- The 2019 Global Consensus Statement specifically states: "compounded bioidentical testosterone therapy cannot be recommended" due to "insufficient evidence of efficacy and safety." Compounding introduces pharmacokinetic variability not present in standardized formulations.
- **Evidence quality: Level 2 (consensus guideline based on systematic evidence review). The statement reflects absence of RCT data using compounded preparations rather than evidence of harm, but the regulatory and pharmacokinetic concern is legitimate.**

**Misleh's position (uses compounded creams and injections):**
- Compounded testosterone is his primary delivery method, used routinely in clinical practice and presumably taught in his course.
- Many HRT clinicians outside guideline-compliant endocrinology practices use compounded testosterone due to the absence of FDA-approved female testosterone preparations in the US (as of this writing). This is a real-world limitation that the guidelines partially acknowledge.
- **Evidence quality: Expert opinion + common practice in integrative/functional medicine. The use of compounded preparations is driven by absence of approved alternatives, not by evidence supporting compounding specifically.**

**Verdict on Divergence 4:** The consensus concern about compounding is regulatory and pharmacokinetic rather than evidence of active harm. In practice, most US clinicians prescribing female testosterone use compounded preparations because no FDA-approved female formulation exists in the US. Misleh's use of compounded preparations is consistent with widespread off-label practice, but not with guideline recommendations. Practical and regulatory risk (batch variation, potency errors, lack of pharmacokinetic standardization) is real but unquantified.

---

### Point of Agreement: Anti-Pellet Stance

Both Misleh and the 2019 consensus advise against testosterone pellets in women. The reasons are aligned but stated differently:
- Misleh (public content): Pellets cannot be reversed if side effects emerge; reversibility and adjustability are clinically necessary.
- Consensus: Pellets and high-dose injectables produce supraphysiologic testosterone concentrations; no RCT evidence; risk of irreversible androgenic effects.

**Evidence quality for anti-pellet position:** Level 1–2. The 2019 Lancet meta-analysis included no RCTs of pellet therapy because none met inclusion criteria (partly reflecting the pharmacokinetic issues). The risk of supraphysiologic levels with pellets is well-documented in observational and pharmacokinetic data.
